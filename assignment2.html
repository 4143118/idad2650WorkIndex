Pencil Hover Feedback

My first area of exploration is how Pencil Jam helps children recognise that the pencils are interactive. The instrument is designed for young children, so I want them to explore the page naturally without relying too heavily on written instructions. Pencils are familiar objects, but children may not immediately realise that the pencils on the screen can be picked up and used to make music. Alongside basic written prompts, I therefore want to give the pencils visual feedback. The three prototypes keep the pencil interaction the same and change only the visual response when the cursor moves over a pencil. I want to find out which response users notice first, how they interpret it, and whether it encourages them to try dragging the pencil.

Prototype A1: Slight Lift

When hovered over, the pencil lifts slightly. This movement is intended to make it feel as though the pencil is waiting to be picked up. I want to find out whether this physical cue makes children more likely to think of dragging it.

Prototype A2: White Highlight

When hovered over, a white outline appears around the pencil to highlight it. I want to find out whether children understand this highlight as a sign that the pencil is interactive and can be used to make sound.

Prototype A3: Slight Lift & White Highlight

When hovered over, the pencil lifts slightly and is surrounded by a white outline. I want to find out whether combining the two visual cues makes the pencil easier to notice, or whether the feedback becomes too much.

Ruler Speed Control

My second area of exploration is how children interact with the speed control. The ruler fits the stationery theme, but children need to discover through interaction that it controls the music’s speed. I want to find out whether they notice that using the ruler changes the speed of the loop, and how different interaction methods affect their sense of control. All three prototypes offer slow, medium and fast settings. They differ in how mouse movement triggers a change and whether the user needs to confirm their choice. This allows me to focus the comparison on the interaction method itself.

Prototype B1: Immediate Speed Change

As the cursor moves across the ruler, the music’s speed changes immediately. Children can move between positions and hear the corresponding effect. I want to find out whether this immediate feedback helps them connect their movement with the change in sound.

Prototype B2: Drag to Set the Speed

Children drag along the ruler to choose a speed. When they release the mouse, the music continues playing at the selected speed. Dragging makes changing the speed a deliberate action. I want to find out whether this method gives children a clearer sense that they are choosing and setting the speed.

Prototype B3: Hover to Preview, Click to Confirm

When the cursor hovers over a section of the ruler, children can preview the corresponding speed. They click to confirm their choice. This gives them a chance to hear an option before deciding. I want to explore whether separating preview from confirmation helps children understand the three speed settings, or adds an unnecessary step.
