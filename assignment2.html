Information Design and Learnability of the Pencils

My first area of exploration is how Pencil Jam helps children recognise that the pencils are interactive. The instrument is designed for young children, so I want them to explore the page naturally without relying too heavily on written instructions. Pencils are familiar objects, but children may not immediately realise that the pencils on the screen can be picked up and used to make music. Alongside basic written prompts, I therefore want to give the pencils visual feedback. The three prototypes keep the pencil interaction the same and change only the visual response when the cursor moves over a pencil. I want to find out which response users notice first, how they interpret it, and whether it encourages them to try dragging the pencil.

Mapping Mouse Movement to Speed

My second area of exploration is how children interact with the ruler to control the music’s speed. I chose mouse movement as my extended technique because it allows children to explore the ruler through movement and hear how their actions affect the loop. The ruler fits the stationery theme, but children need to discover through interaction that it controls the music’s speed. I want to find out whether they notice that using the ruler changes the speed of the loop, and how different interaction methods affect their sense of control. All three prototypes offer slow, medium and fast settings. They differ in how mouse movement triggers a change and whether the user needs to confirm their choice. This allows me to focus the comparison on the interaction method itself.
